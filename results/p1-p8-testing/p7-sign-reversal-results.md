# Test P7: Sign Reversal on Generative Substrates

## Do lineages with cumulative culture show positive diversity-dependent speciation?

**Date:** 2026-08-18
**Task:** Systematic literature survey examining whether cultural bird lineages (corvids, parrots, etc.) exhibit positive diversity-dependent (DD) speciation, in contrast to the negative DD expected under ecological niche limitation.

---

## 1. Core Prediction

The task tests whether the attractor dynamics of diversity-dependent speciation reverse sign when the niche substrate shifts from ecological (finite, bounded) to cultural (generative, expandable). The specific prediction: **lineages with cumulative culture should show positive DD speciation** — speciation rates that *increase* as species diversity grows — mirroring the pattern van Holstein & Foley (2024) found in the genus *Homo*.

---

## 2. Reference Pattern: van Holstein & Foley (2024)

**Paper:** van Holstein, L.A. & Foley, R.A. (2024). Diversity-dependent speciation and extinction in hominins. *Nature Ecology & Evolution*, 8, 1180–1190. DOI: 10.1038/s41559-024-02390-z

**Key findings:**
- **Hominins overall:** Speciation was negatively diversity-dependent (consistent with ecological niche limitation)
- **Australopithecus & Paranthropus:** Negative DD (same pattern as most vertebrates)
- **Homo:** **Positively diversity-dependent speciation** and **negatively diversity-dependent extinction** — described as "exceedingly rare patterns across all forms of life"
- The authors attribute this to "repeated *Homo* range expansions driven by interspecific competition and made possible by recurrent innovations in ecological strategies" — i.e., cultural/technological niche construction

**Previously reported cases of positive DD:** Only island-dwelling beetles (Emerson & Kolm 2005), plants and arthropods (contested). *Homo* is the first vertebrate lineage shown to exhibit positive DD speciation.

## 4. Cultural Lineages in Birds: Evidence for Cumulative Culture

### Corvidae (crows, ravens, jays) — ~135 spp.
- **Tool use:** New Caledonian crows (*Corvus moneduloides*) show cumulative cultural evolution in tool manufacture — hook-shaped tools refined over generations (Hunt & Gray 2003; Jelbert et al. 2018)
- **Social learning:** Complex social networks, transmission of foraging innovations across generations
- **Cognitive capacity:** Brain-to-body ratios comparable to higher primates
- **Vocal learning:** Limited compared to parrots, but complex vocal repertoires with regional dialects

### Psittacidae (parrots) — ~179 spp. (true parrots), ~410 spp. (order Psittaciformes)
- **Vocal learning:** Open-ended vocal learning, dialect formation, cultural transmission of song
- **Tool use:** Documented in Goffins cockatoos and kea, including cumulative refinement
- **Social traditions:** Regional foraging techniques, food preferences transmitted culturally

### Ptilonorhynchidae (bowerbirds) — ~20 spp.
- **Cumulative bower-building:** Complex, learned bower structures with cultural transmission of decoration preferences
- Strong sexual selection driving cultural elaboration

### Trochilidae (hummingbirds) — ~360 spp.
- **Vocal learning:** Yes, but limited cumulative culture
- Primarily vocal learning without strong evidence of cumulative cultural evolution

### Picidae (woodpeckers) — ~240 spp.
- Some tool use (e.g., acorn woodpeckers), but limited cumulative culture

### Non-cultural comparison groups
- **Galliformes** (game birds, ~300 spp.): No vocal learning, limited social learning
- **Anseriformes** (waterfowl, ~170 spp.): Limited vocal learning, no tool use, no cumulative culture

## 5. Published Speciation Dynamics in Cultural Bird Lineages

### 5.1 Corvidae — The clearest test case

**Key paper:** Garcia-Porta et al. (2022). Niche expansion and adaptive divergence in the global radiation of crows and ravens. *Nature Communications*, 13, 2086.

**Critical findings:**
- **Corvidae overall:** Shows a **decay in speciation rates over time** — consistent with negative DD (ecological niche saturation)
- **Corvus (crows and ravens):** Shows a **secondary peak of speciation around 10 Ma** that interrupts the general decay
- The BAMM analysis found a single rate increase at the base of *Corvus* (frequency = 0.36) or at the node separating *Corvus* from *Coloeus* (frequency = 0.28)
- Speciation rates in *Corvus* are "at least double the mean background diversification rate" of the rest of Corvidae
- The secondary peak coincides with massive climatic niche expansion and increased phenotypic diversification
- **Interpretation:** The secondary peak in *Corvus* could be interpreted as a "release" from negative DD rather than true positive DD. The overall pattern in Corvidae remains negative DD (decay over time), but *Corvus* shows a transient deviation.

**Direct quote from Garcia-Porta et al. (2022):**
> "We observe a decay in speciation rates over time interrupted by a secondary peak of speciation around 10 Ma, the estimated time of origin of *Corvus*."

### 5.2 Psittacidae — Relatively constant rates

**Key findings:**
- Parrots are characterized by **relatively constant diversification rates** throughout their evolutionary history
- Described as having **"slow velocities of reproductive isolation evolution"** compared to other bird families (Rabosky & Matute 2013)
- Major diversification of Psittaciformes occurred around 40 Ma, following separation of Australia from West Antarctica and South America
- **No evidence of positive DD:** The constant-rate pattern suggests diversity-independent speciation, not positive DD

### 5.3 Vocal learning and speciation rates

**Key paper:** Mason, N.A. & Burns, K.J. (2017). Song evolution, speciation, and vocal learning in passerine birds. *Evolution*, 71, 786–796.

**Findings:**
- Evolutionary bursts in speciation and song evolution occurred **coincidentally** in both oscines (learned song) and suboscines (innate song)
- Overall rates of vocal evolution were **higher in taxa with learned songs** (oscines) than innate songs (suboscines)
- **But:** This did not translate to higher speciation rates in vocal learners — the relationship between vocal learning and speciation rate is complex
- A "downside of learning" exists: greater variability in learned songs may require larger absolute acoustic differences to achieve the same level of reproductive isolation

### 5.4 Cumulative cultural evolution in bird song

**Key paper:** Williams, H. et al. (2022). Cumulative cultural evolution and mechanisms for cultural selection in wild bird songs. *Nature Communications*, 13, 4001.

**Findings:**
- Documented cumulative cultural evolution in Savannah sparrow songs over 30+ years
- "Click trains" replaced "high note clusters," then were elaborated with more clicks
- The new songs elicited stronger behavioral responses from both males and females
- **Single species study:** Demonstrates the mechanism exists but cannot speak to macroevolutionary DD patterns

## 6. Results: Testing the Prediction

### 6.1 Was the prediction supported?

**NO — the prediction that cultural bird lineages should show positive DD speciation is NOT supported by existing published data.**

| Lineage | Culture | Published DD pattern | Evidence for positive DD? |
|---------|---------|---------------------|--------------------------|
| Corvidae (overall) | Cumulative tool use, social learning | Negative DD (decay in speciation rates) | No |
| *Corvus* (crows/ravens) | Advanced cumulative culture | Secondary peak interrupting negative DD | **Partial/ambiguous** — deviation from negative DD, but not formally tested as positive DD |
| Psittacidae | Vocal learning, tool use | Constant-rate (diversity-independent) | No |
| Oscine passerines (general) | Vocal learning | Mixed — some clades show negative DD, coincident bursts with suboscines | No |
| Suboscine passerines | Innate song | Same coincident bursts as oscines | No |
| Galliformes | No cumulative culture | Negative DD (presumed) | No (consistent with prediction) |

### 6.2 The *Corvus* anomaly

The secondary peak in *Corvus* is the **closest thing to a positive DD signal** in the bird literature:
- Decay in speciation rates (negative DD) is the background pattern in Corvidae
- *Corvus* shows a secondary peak that interrupts this decay
- Garcia-Porta et al. attribute this to "traits that, like cognition, facilitate persistence in new environments"
- **However:** This is a **secondary peak within an overall negative DD framework**, not a formal demonstration of positive DD
- The authors did not fit positive DD models — they used BAMM (which detects rate shifts) and pulled speciation rates (which are time-dependent, not diversity-dependent)

### 6.3 What would a formal test require?

A proper test of P7 would require:

1. **Phylogenetic data:** Download the Jetz et al. (2012) phylogeny from birdtree.org (10,000 trees, 9,993 species)
2. **Clade extraction:** Extract cultural lineages (Corvidae, Psittacidae, Ptilonorhynchidae) and non-cultural comparison lineages (Galliformes, Anseriformes, Tinamiformes)
3. **DD model fitting:** Use the DDD R package (Etienne & Haegeman 2012) to fit:
   - Constant-rate model (no diversity dependence)
   - Negative DD model (speciation decreases with diversity)
   - Positive DD model (speciation increases with diversity)
4. **Model comparison:** Use AIC or likelihood ratio tests to compare models

**Constraints:** The DDD package requires subtrees in nexus format. The birdtree.org phylogenies are available as nexus files, but each is a distribution of 10,000 trees (not a single consensus tree). Analysis would require computing over the posterior distribution.

## 7. Discussion

### 7.1 Why the prediction failed

Several factors may explain why positive DD is not observed in cultural bird lineages:

1. **Scale of cultural niche construction:** Bird culture, while impressive, operates at a much smaller scale than human cumulative culture. No bird species has built a global niche comparable to *Homo*'s technological niche construction.

2. **Geographic vs. cultural speciation:** In birds, geographic isolation remains the dominant speciation mechanism. Culture may accelerate premating isolation (through song divergence) but does not fundamentally alter the geography of speciation.

3. **The "downside of learning":** Mason & Burns (2017) found that learned songs may actually be *less* effective at driving reproductive isolation because variability within learned songs makes species boundaries fuzzier.

4. **Ecological constraints remain primary:** Even in cognitively advanced birds, ecological limits (food availability, nesting sites, competition) still constrain diversity. Culture does not generate unlimited new niche space in the way that *Homo*'s technology did.

### 7.2 Implications for the framework under review

The failure of P7 suggests that **sign reversal on generative substrates is not automatic** — it requires a threshold of cultural niche construction that birds have not reached. This is consistent with the framework's predictions if:

- Cultural niche construction operates on a continuum, not a binary
- Only lineages that cross a threshold of "generative capacity" exhibit sign reversal
- *Homo* may be the only lineage to have crossed this threshold among vertebrates

### 7.3 Alternative: Cultural complexity and speciation rate

While positive DD is not supported, there is evidence that **cultural complexity correlates with higher speciation rates** in some bird lineages:
- Corvidae shows elevated speciation rates compared to its background clade
- Vocal learning lineages show higher rates of vocal evolution
- But this is rate elevation, not true positive DD

### 7.4 Future work

A formal test using the DDD R package on the Jetz et al. (2012) phylogeny would be the definitive test. The necessary data and tools exist:
- **Data:** birdtree.org phylogenies (10,000 trees, 9,993 species)
- **Tool:** DDD R package (Etienne & Haegeman 2012)
- **Method:** Fit positive DD, negative DD, and constant-rate models to cultural vs. non-cultural clades
- **Prediction (revised):** Cultural lineages may show *weaker* negative DD (i.e., slower decay) rather than true positive DD
